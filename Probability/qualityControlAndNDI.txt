Question:
Quality Control & NDI — Probability of Defectives in a Sample

Question

Suppose we have a large inventory Say

n = total number of items in the lot
k = total number of defective items in the lot

We randomly take a small sample r:

r = sample size
ACTUAL QUESTION : 
If the sample contains exactly m defective items, 
what is the probability (likelihood) that this 
particular sample can occur when we randomly 
select r items from the large lot of n items 
containing k defectives?

Important: What are we actually finding?

We are NOT trying to find:
how many defective items are in the inventory
how many defectives should exist

Those values are already given/assumed.
We are finding:

The probability of occurrence of a sample of size r 
containing exactly m defectives, 
when it is taken from a larger lot containing n items 
and k defectives.

In other words:
Out of all possible samples of size r that 
we could take from the large inventory, 
what percentage of those possible samples would 
contain exactly m defectives?


answer:
 p(m defective in r sample) = (kCm)((n-k)C(r-m))
                              -------------------
                                    nCr

The denominator is obviously ncr, the number of ways
sample r can be chosen in whole inventory n

the nominator part is divided in to two parts
-> finding m defective (intersected with k) which can be found
   in multiple ways of k
-> finding r-m non defective (intersected with n-k)
   which can be found in multiple ways of n-k

Multiplying the both defective and non-defective parts give us
all the ways the sample r can be found in inventory n

Calculating the probability of finding m=2 defectives 
in a small sample of r=5 allows you to make statistical 
decisions about an entire shipment without inspecting all 
N=100 items—a foundational practice in quality control known 
as Acceptance Sampling.

1. Testing Hypotheses About Lot Quality
In the real world, you do not actually know 
K(the true number of defectives in the warehouse batch). 
A supplier might claim K = 10 (a 10% defect rate). 
If you pull r = 5 items and find m = 2 defectives, you 
plug K = 10 into the hypergeometric formula and get a 
probability of roughly 7%. Because pulling 2 defectives 
out of 5 is such a low-probability event under a 10% defect 
rate, this result warns you that the batch is almost certainly 
worse than the supplier claimed, giving you mathematical 
justification to reject the shipment.

2. Setting Accept/Reject Rules
Quality control engineers use these probability 
distributions to establish inspection rules before 
touching a shipment 
(e.g., "Inspect r = 5 items; accept the lot if m<=1, reject if m>=2"). 
Calculating the exact probabilities helps manage two 
critical operational risks:

Consumer's Risk: The probability of accidentally 
accepting a bad batch because a random sample happened to 
look okay.
Producer's Risk: The probability of accidentally rejecting a 
good batch because a random sample happened to hit a rare 
clump of defects.

3. Saving Time and Inspection Costs
When testing is destructive 
(e.g., crash-testing components, stress-testing materials) 
or labor-intensive (NDI ultrasonic scans), 
inspecting 100% of a batch is impossible or cost-prohibitive. 
Calculating the hypergeometric probability tells you how much 
statistical confidence you gain from a sample of 5 versus a 
sample of 20.
